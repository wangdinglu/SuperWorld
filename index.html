Design rule 1

The platform owns physics, networking and safety. Players and agents only author content and behaviour inside a sandbox.

Design rule 2

Every scene is stored as data, not code. The same world renders in 3D on a laptop, in 2D on a small phone, and later in a native app.

Getting around

One camera, three zoom levels: walk, overview, atlas

Nobody has to walk far. Zoom out to pick where to go, then zoom back in to arrive. The middle level plays like Dota: a small figure on a readable board, tap to move.

Pinch out to rise

OverviewThe district becomes a small, readable board. Tap a spot and your avatar walks there; landmarks and friends are easy to spot.

How a trip feels

  1. Pinch out or press M: the camera rises from your avatar to the overview, then to the atlas.
  2. The atlas shows friends, live events and busy spots. Tap where you want to go.
  3. The camera dives in. The destination's 2D map tile shows first while 3D detail streams, so you never see a blank loading screen.
  4. You land at its arrival point, a few steps from where you aimed.
Fast travel is freebetween public places; walking is for discovery, never a toll.
Every place has a linkthat drops a friend straight in from any browser.
Join a friendfrom their profile or the atlas; parties travel together.
Private spaces need a knock: you land at the door until the owner lets you in.
Places stay worth walking: hidden doors, puzzle clues and chance meetings reward anyone who wanders.
Safe arrival: you pass through others for a moment, so nobody lands on anyone.

Each district is its own server room, so a trip is a room switch hidden by the zoom. 2D mode is simply the overview camera kept on.

Rule of thumb: if a feature changes how the world runs, it belongs in the engine. If it changes what is in the world, players and agents define it. A new item, track or room never needs an engine release.

Part by part, following the two design rules and the web-first goal.

Agents and Creator SDK

The agent plans and talks; specialist models make meshes; your SDK checks everything

One LLM agent drives creation through a typed SDK, published as an MCP server so any model can use it. It never writes raw code that runs on other players' devices.

Avatar pipeline

Owned end to end. Ready Player Me, once the default service, shut down on 31 Jan 2026.
  1. Step 1

    Describe

    Type or say how you want to look, or upload a picture.

  2. Step 2

    Concept

    The agent writes a character brief and shows a concept image to approve.

  3. Step 3

    Mesh

    Image-to-3D builds the body; humanoids are auto-rigged and animated.

  4. Step 4

    Fit

    Retarget to one skeleton, shrink for phones, save as VRM 1.0 in your storage.

  5. Gate

    Check

    Moderation checks mesh and textures before public zones.

Building agentEdits your space through the SDK: place objects, generate assets, add behaviours, screens, lighting, music and who may visit. You see a live preview and accept or undo each step.
AI charactersThe same agents run hosts and guides: a museum curator, a race marshal, a librarian. They run on your servers, so you control their cost and behaviour.
ItemsA 3D model plus settings for one engine behaviour: wear, hold, sit, throw, drive, play or open a screen. A new item never needs new code.

Library first, custom shader second

One TSL source runs on both WebGPU and WebGL2. Compute-only nodes stay off the list because the WebGL2 fallback cannot run them.

One schema, three doors

Generate all three from the same tool definitions so they never drift apart.

API

Build first

The source of truth. The game and your in-game agent use it, the agent through Claude's tool use.

MCP server

Second

For outside agents: players' own AI, Claude apps and other models can build without custom code.

CLI

Third

For power creators, coding agents, scripts and CI: bulk imports, batch edits, automated tests.

Why VRM for avatars

VRM 1.0 is glTF plus a standard humanoid skeleton, facial expressions and lip-sync, eye gaze, spring bones for hair and clothes, and licence metadata saying who may use the avatar. Cap spring bones in crowds, let the style system override VRM's anime-leaning toon shader, and convert plain rigged GLB uploads to VRM in the pipeline.

When styles meet

    

      
The place sets the light
Lighting, sky and post-effects belong to the place, so a visiting avatar is lit and colour-graded by it. That alone blends most mixes.

      
The owner chooses
A space keeps visitor styles by default, like the OASIS, or harmonises them with its own outlines and colour treatment.

      
Portals mark the change
Moving between differently styled zones plays a short transition, so the change feels deliberate.

      
Crowds stay light
Busy public zones use library materials. Full custom avatar shaders show in private spaces and up close.

    

    
At launch, players can describe a style any way they like. Style rules are revisited together with the copyright filters.

How a studio session runs

    
    
      
  1. Start from an idea: a mood, a word, a picture, or “surprise me”.
    2. 
      
  2. The agent proposes a few drafts (a room, an item, an avatar), each with its own style profile.
    3. 
      
  3. You walk into each draft; the next one is generated in the background while you look.
    4. 
      
  4. You steer by talking: “more like this”, “calmer”, “warmer light”. The agent edits through the Creator SDK.
    5. 
      
  5. Keep what works: place it in your private space, save it to your library, or submit it to a district or the museum.
    6.

Multiplayer and screens

      
Server-authoritative rooms, and screens that play video and real web pages

    

    

      
Hangout zonesEach phone predicts its own movement; the server sends only nearby players, a few times a second.

      
Races and puzzlesThe server decides finish times and solutions, so leaderboards cannot be faked.

      
StorageA database for accounts, inventories and scene files; a CDN for models, sprites and video.

      
VoiceProximity voice through LiveKit, muted by default for new and young accounts.

      
StackColyseus rooms for world state; our own social service, or Nakama, for friends and chat. Photon is skipped: its browser SDK is a client-authoritative relay.

    

    
Three kinds of screen content

    

      
Video
Now
Drawn onto the screen surface. Theatre screenings follow the server clock, so everyone sees the same frame.

      
HTML page
Now
A sandboxed frame on a separate domain, laid over the screen when you walk up. Distant screens show a still snapshot.

      
HTML in the world
Later
Swap the overlay for the HTML-in-Canvas API once it leaves Chrome's origin trial (versions 148 to 150) and other browsers adopt it.

    

    
On phones, tapping any screen opens it full-size as a flat panel. User-made pages never share cookies or account access with the game.

Five ad concepts
20 to 30 second films shot on real gameplay. Lead with concept 2.

    

      
Concept 1
Who will you be?
Borrows the opening transformation. Sells an avatar from one sentence, straight from a link.

        
  1. A tired commuter on a rainy Tokyo train scrolls her phone.
  2. She taps a link and types “a fox knight made of autumn leaves”.
  3. Leaves swirl into her avatar; she steps into a sunny plaza full of people in different art styles.
  4. Back on the train, her reflection in the window is the fox knight. She smiles.

        
Describe yourself. Step in.

      
Concept 2 · lead with this
Say it, walk into it
Borrows stepping inside a story. Sells the creator studio.

        
  1. A girl on the sofa says to her phone, “a library under the sea”.
  2. Cut: she is standing inside it. Fish drift between the shelves; a whale glides past the reading lamps.
  3. “Warmer light,” she says. The water turns gold.
  4. She sends a link, and her grandfather appears beside her from a tablet in another city.

        
If you can say it, you can walk into it.

      
Concept 3
Turn around
Borrows the race won by going backward. Sells races and puzzles with secrets.

        
  1. A roaring race through a city that rebuilds itself around the cars.
  2. Every racer hits the same wall and respawns.
  3. One player stops, spots a tiny clue on a billboard, and turns around.
  4. The leaderboard shows one name.

        
Every track hides a secret.

      
Concept 4
Ask the museum
Borrows Halliday's Journals. Sells the museum and its AI curator.

        
  1. A hall of glowing windows, each holding a scene a player made.
  2. A visitor asks the curator, “Who made this, and why?”
  3. The scene rewinds to show its making, from the first sentence to the finished room.
  4. The visitor opens the studio to make one of their own.

        
Every exhibit has a story. Ask it.

      
Concept 5
Everyone shows up
Borrows the final gathering. Sells live events and a world of mixed styles.

        
  1. A countdown on the plaza screens. Players arrive from phones on buses and laptops in cafes.
  2. Thousands of avatars, each in its own art style, fill the plaza.
  3. A creature a child built in the studio rises over the crowd as fireworks go off.
  4. Wide shot: the whole world in one frame.

        
Launch night. Come as who you want to be.

    


    
A real launch hunt: “The first key”

    
The precedent: for the 2012 paperback, Ernest Cline hid an Easter egg in the text. It led to a new Atari 2600 game, then staged challenges on 1 July and 1 August, and the winner, Craig Queen, was handed a restored DeLorean on G4's X-Play.

    
    
      
  1. The final frame of the launch film hides a clue.
    2. 
      
  2. The clue opens a hidden door in the culture district.
    3. 
      
  3. Three gates, one per district: a race whose shortcut hides in plain sight, a library riddle the AI curator will only hint at, and a studio brief judged by the community.
    4. 
      
  4. Plaza screens show a live leaderboard, so the hunt itself becomes something to watch.
    5. 
      
  5. The winner gets a real-world prize and a permanent exhibit in the museum; everyone who finishes keeps a badge item.
    6. 
    

    
Run it for a fixed window and end with the “Everyone shows up” launch night. Prize promotions are regulated, and Japan caps the value of some promotional prizes, so have the rules checked for each country.

      Never name itno “Ready Player One”, “OASIS” or character names in ads, store listings or slogans

      Show what shipsanything AI-made in an ad can be made in the product that day
